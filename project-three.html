Foody Foody is a cross-platform food rescue service that rethinks how surplus meals can be redistributed before they become waste. Instead of presenting rescued food as a secondary or inconvenient option, the project reframes it as a clear, trustworthy, and repeatable ordering experience across three connected touchpoints: a consumer app, a lightweight LINE flow, and a merchant dashboard.

Project Snapshot

What this project demonstrates

Foody Foody is presented as a service design case study rather than a standalone UI exercise. The work moves from food-waste research into opportunity framing, product structure, journey design, and a multi-touchpoint ordering system that balances user convenience with restaurant feasibility.

Motivation

In 2021, the UN Food and Agriculture Organization highlighted a critical disparity: while 828 million people globally suffered from hunger, nearly one-third of all produced food was wasted. This alarming gap inspired us to seek solutions that reduce food waste and align with the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) for responsible consumption and production.

Problem framing

Why This Problem Matters

Surplus meals often go unsold not only because of timing, but because the experience around them feels uncertain. People may not know what is still available, how much food they will actually get, or whether the pickup effort will be worth it. Restaurants, meanwhile, need a lightweight way to publish and manage rescued meals without adding friction to daily operations.

Portfolio PositioningFor a hiring case study, the value of this project is not only the sustainability topic itself, but the way the service translates ambiguity into clearer product structure, interface logic, and cross-platform coordination.

The design challenge was to reduce hesitation. That meant making rescued meals easier to discover, easier to trust, and easier to reserve before they disappeared from the daily food cycle.

The stronger direction was to treat food rescue as a product-system problem rather than a message campaign. Once the work was framed that way, the app, LINE flow, and merchant tools could be designed as one connected service instead of three separate interfaces.

The early research highlighted two linked realities: edible food is repeatedly discarded in small everyday moments, and most of that waste happens long before people ever see it as an option. The problem therefore was not awareness alone, but the absence of a usable redistribution mechanism.

The concept was scoped near National Taiwan Normal University so the proposal could stay grounded in a believable urban routine. Nearby restaurants, student ordering habits, and rescue timing were treated as the real terrain the design needed to serve.

Customer Analysis Survey

The survey covered 167 students from National Taiwan Normal University and focused on meal ordering habits, rescued-food acceptance, and the role of portion control in purchase decisions. The charts below convert the original PDF graphics into portfolio-ready HTML while keeping the data intact.

Rescued-meal buying already exists as a familiar behavior for most respondents, which means the design challenge is not inventing a new habit from nothing, but reducing hesitation and making the behavior feel easier and more trustworthy.

Ordering System Users

Yes · 62%
No · 38%

A majority had already used ordering systems, so digital behavior is not the main barrier. Friction comes more from poor information, awkward timing, and low confidence in what will actually be received.

Custom Portion Size Boosts Purchase Intent

Yes · 93%
No · 7%

This is one of the clearest signals in the deck. Portion flexibility does not function as a minor feature. It changes willingness to buy, which is why adjustable quantity becomes central to the concept.

Meal Choice Factors

Size · 62%
Price · 38%

Respondents cared more about getting a manageable meal size at the same price than simply ordering the regular portion. The implication is sharp: value perception is tied to fit, not only to cost.

Ordering Behavior

  • Ordering channels: LINE 40%, app 33%, phone 21%, delivery platforms 5%, website 1%.
  • Pre-order timing clustered around one hour before meals, two hours before meals, and next-day advance ordering.
  • The most commonly ordered meal type was lunch boxes, followed by desserts, fast food, breakfast shops, and beverages.

Why People Buy or Avoid Leftovers

  • Main motivations for buying leftovers were saving money and being eco-conscious.
  • Main barriers were low leftover visibility, face-consciousness, fear of stale food, and the time needed to search for suitable items.
  • According to the survey, 15% often have leftover meals themselves, with appetite uncertainty and dieting among the main reasons.

From research to product structure

Service Strategy

Design OpportunityThe service had to reduce hesitation for consumers while keeping publishing, quantity control, and reservation handling manageable for stores. The strategy therefore focused on clarity, low-friction ordering, and operational visibility at the same time.

Shop Journey Map

Mapped how surplus food appears in daily restaurant operations and where staff decision points emerge.

Survey Insights

Used questionnaire data to understand ordering expectations, trust barriers, and portion-related hesitation.

Personas

Defined multiple user motivations, including convenience-driven, value-driven, and sustainability-driven behavior.

Ordering Journey

Clarified the sequence from discovery to reservation, pickup, and post-order reassurance.

Feature Scope

Focused the concept on local discovery, portion clarity, reward logic, and merchant-side coordination.

The resulting strategy was to make food rescue feel visible, desirable, and ordinary rather than charitable or leftover in tone. Recommendation, environmental feedback, and reward cues were used to support adoption, but the real goal was to make rescued meals feel like a normal part of local ordering behavior.

On the store side, the service needed to remain operationally realistic. Publishing meals, adjusting quantity, managing pickup windows, and reviewing order patterns were treated as part of one coordinated system, which is why the project extends beyond consumer UI into LINE and backend tooling.

Address food waste from both consumers and businesses

The service is positioned as a bridge between demand uncertainty on the store side and appetite uncertainty on the user side, so waste reduction is built into everyday ordering instead of treated as a separate campaign.

The system turns leftover food into visible inventory, giving stores a second sales window before food becomes disposal, and giving users a clearer way to absorb that surplus.

3

Lower waste means fewer ingredients, packaging, and logistics are spent on meals that never get eaten. The design therefore treats operational clarity as an environmental lever.

Stores recover value, users access affordable meals, and the platform normalizes circular food behavior. That loop is the service model the selection PDF keeps pointing toward.

Outcome & Reflection

Foody Foody demonstrates how a sustainability problem can be translated into a service architecture with multiple points of entry. The project links food rescue with routine ordering behavior so surplus redistribution feels like part of an existing food ecosystem rather than a separate moral action.

The key design tension was between idealism and usability. The work became stronger when the service prioritized convenience, visibility, and trust first, then allowed environmental value to emerge through repeated use and accumulated feedback.

Next Steps

ReflectionIf I had more time and resources, I would push the project from strong concept definition into real-world pilot validation, focusing on whether rescued meal discovery, reservation timing, and restaurant participation remain clear under actual operational pressure.

Pilot With Local Stores

Run a small launch with nearby restaurants to observe publishing frequency, pickup behavior, and whether rescue inventory can be updated reliably in practice.

Task-Based Usability Testing

Impact Tracking
